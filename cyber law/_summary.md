

### The legal system
- **Legal System Goals**: Organization (community, state, law), conflict prevention/regulation, policy guidelines, binding relationships, balance competing interests.  
- **Players**: Law, courts, legal scholars, international law firms.  
- **Architecture**: Legal systems (Civil, Common Law), regulatory centers, binding/abstract rules, sources (purpose, hierarchy, scope).  
- **Coordination**: International (treaties/conventions), EU (treaties, directives, regulations), National (constitution, statutes, regulations), soft-law.  
- **Conflict Resolution**: Hierarchy (ECJ, constitutional courts), Lex posterior (new > old), Lex specialis (specific > general).  

### Rule making


- **Rule-Making**: New laws for technology (costs, systemic risks, past experience). Complementary approach (e.g., EU e-commerce regulation).  
- **Regulation Approaches**:  
  - **Prescriptive**: Clear rules, compliance, sanctions (pros: flexible; cons: costly enforcement).  
  - **Preventive**: Embedded in technology (pros: proactive; cons: inflexible).  
  - **Combination**: Example—security settings (preventive) + hacking laws (prescriptive).  

- **Rule-Making Styles**:  
  - **Top-Down**: Hard law (effective but slow to adapt).  
  - **Bottom-Up**: Soft law (codes of conduct, flexible, weak enforcement).  
  - **Co-Regulation**: Mix of both.  

- **Regulatory Balance**:  
  - **Principles-Based**: Flexible but uncertain.  
  - **Detailed Rules**: Clear but outdated quickly.  
  - **Hybrid**: Principles + standards = adaptable and specific.  

- **Technology Risks**:  
	- **Collingridge Dilemma**: 
	  - **Early Stages**: Risks unpredictable for new technology.
	  - **Late Stages**: Regulation challenging once technology is widespread.
	- **Regulating Uncertainty**:  
	  - **Risk Management**: Preventive actions for uncertain but harmful risks.  
	  - **Precautionary Principle**: Assess/mitigate risks without halting technology.  
- **Socio-Technical Dynamics**: Technology ↔ societal values, globalization challenges regulation.  
- **Stakeholders**:  
  - **Individuals**: Privacy, freedom, but overlook collective issues.  
  - **Communities**: Participation ensures acceptability.  
  - **States**: Balance innovation and public interest.  
  - **Businesses**: Prefer self-regulation, co-regulate with governments.  

- **Liability Rules**:  
  - **Fault**: Liability with proven fault.  
  - **Strict**: Liability regardless of fault (e.g., high-risk sectors).  
  - **State Role**: Covers extreme cases (e.g., nuclear disasters).  
  - **Insurance**: Mitigates liability costs.  

- **Trade-Off**: Innovation vs. protection costs.  

### Law & Technology Design

- **Design-Focused Approach**:  
  - **By-Design**: Opportunity, values, expertise, policymaker influence.  

- **Design Creation**:  
  - **Risk-Based**: Prevention/mitigation.  
  - **Rights-Based**: Social/law focus.  

- **Regulation Drivers**:  
  - **Harm-Based**: Market-oriented (cost/benefit focus).  
  - **Rights-Based**: Social/legal emphasis.  

- **By-Design Limitations**:  
  - Legal changes, territorial scope, lack of transparency, market fragmentation, user impact (transparency/freedom).  


## DATA PROTECTION REGULATION

### Fundamentals

- **Privacy Evolution**:  
  - **US**: Privacy from property to personality right; private → constitutional law (informational/decisional privacy).  
  - **Europe**: Informational privacy origins in copyright, moral rights, and constitutional declarations.  

- **EU Data Protection**:  
  - **First Generation**: Digitalization era, counter-control over information, procedural approach, lack of clear legal ground.  
  - **Second Generation**: Fundamental rights focus (Article 8 ECHR), rights-based vs. risk/benefit analysis, licensing → notifications.  
  - **Third Generation**: Harmonization (OECD, Convention 108), economic value of data, consent as legal ground/risk self-assessment, Directive 95/46/EC.  
  - **Fourth Generation**: GDPR, risk management focus (accountability), AI challenges (consent, purpose limitation, minimization, safeguarded interests).  

### Key elements of data protection regulations

- **Personal Data (GDPR)**:  
  - Any information relating to an identified/identifiable person.  
  - Broad definition (e.g., IP address, smart devices, facial recognition).  
  - Article 4: Includes identifiers (e.g., name, ID, online data, socio-economic/psychological traits).  

- **Anonymous Data**:  
  - GDPR Recital 26: Not subject to GDPR if irreversibly anonymized.  
  - Factors: Costs, time, current/future technology (risk of re-identification).  

- **Pseudonymisation (Art. 4)**:  
	- **Definition**: Personal data processed so it cannot be attributed to an individual without additional, separately kept information.
	- **Conditions**: Additional information must be secured with technical/organizational measures to prevent identification.

- **Sensitive Data (Art. 9)**:  
  - Categories: Race, religion, health, sexual orientation, genetics, biometrics.  
  - Requires special safeguards.  

- **Data Processing (Art. 4)**:  
  - Includes collection, storage, organization, modification, transmission, deletion, etc.  
  - Exclusion: Personal/household activities by private individuals.  

- **Key Legal Frameworks**:  
  - Regulation (EU) 2018/1807 (non-personal data).  
  - Special focus: Aggregate/encrypted data, pseudonymization techniques, risk-based assessment.  



## GDPR
### scope

- **GDPR Scope**:  
  - **Objectives**: Data protection and free movement of data.  
  - **Article 1**: Protects fundamental rights, ensuring free movement of personal data within the EU.  

- **Material Scope (Article 2)**:  
  - Applies to personal data processing (automated/non-automated).  
  - **Exclusions**: Law enforcement, military intelligence, purely personal activities.  

- **Territorial Scope (Article 3)**:  
  - **Applies to**: Processing by EU-based controllers/processors, regardless of location.  
  - **Non-EU entities**: If targeting EU data subjects or monitoring their behavior within the Union.  
  - **Establishment Criteria**: Broad interpretation for online service providers (stable arrangement, even one agent can qualify).




### Key Principles of GDPR:

1. **Lawfulness, Fairness, and Transparency (Article 5.1.a)**  
   - **Lawful**: Data processing must have clear legal grounds.  
   - **Fair**: Processing must align with reasonable expectations of the data subject.  
   - **Transparent**: Clear and accessible information about data processing must be provided.  

2. **Purpose Limitation (Article 5.1.b)**  
   - Data must be collected for specified, legitimate purposes and cannot be used for incompatible purposes.  
   - Exceptions: Further processing for archiving, research, or public interest is allowed under specific safeguards (Article 89).  
   - **Compatibility Test (Article 6.4)**: Assess if new processing purposes are compatible by evaluating:
     - Purpose link, context, nature of data, consequences, and safeguards.

3. **Data Minimisation (Article 5.1.c)**  
   - Only collect data that is necessary for the specified purpose.  
   - Limit data processing to what is strictly required.  

4. **Accuracy (Article 5.1.d)**  
   - Data must be accurate and updated as necessary.  
   - **Inaccurate data must be rectified or deleted** without delay.  

5. **Storage Limitation (Article 5.1.e)**  
   - Data should not be kept longer than necessary for its original purpose.  
   - Exceptions for archiving, research, or statistical purposes (Article 89) with added safeguards.  

6. **Integrity and Confidentiality (Article 5.1.f)** (Data Security Principle)  
   - Ensure data security by implementing technical and organizational measures to prevent unauthorized access, loss, or damage.  
   - Security measures must be appropriate to the risks posed.  

7. **Accountability (Article 5.2)**  
   - **Controller Responsibility**: Must demonstrate compliance with GDPR.  
   - Implement effective measures to ensure data protection and demonstrate their effectiveness.  
   - **Ex ante analysis**: Conduct compliance checks before any potential violations.  

These principles together ensure a comprehensive approach to personal data protection, focusing on lawful, fair, and secure processing while safeguarding the rights of individuals.
