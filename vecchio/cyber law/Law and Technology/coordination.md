
# Coordination

Here’s an overview of the **coordination of legal sources** at different levels—**international, EU, national**, and the concept of **soft-law**:

### 1. **International Sources**

- **Treaties**: *Formal agreements* between two or more sovereign states or international organizations. Treaties cover a wide range of issues, including trade, human rights, environmental protection, and security. Once ratified, they become binding on the signatory countries, influencing national law. Examples include the **United Nations Charter** and **Paris Agreement on Climate Change**.
    
- **Conventions**: A type of multilateral treaty typically sponsored by international organizations (e.g., the **Geneva Conventions**, **Convention on the Rights of the Child**). Conventions set out internationally agreed-upon standards and are binding once adopted and ratified by states.
    

International law helps coordinate global governance, and states implement these agreements by incorporating them into their national laws.

---

### 2. **European Union Sources**

The EU legal framework works on **harmonization** to ensure consistency of laws among member states, using binding and non-binding legal instruments:

- **Treaties**: These form the foundation of the EU’s legal system. The most significant treaties are the **Treaty on European Union (TEU)** and the **Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU)**. They set out the EU’s structure, its competencies, and the principles guiding EU law.
    
- **Directives**: These are legislative *acts that set out goals* all EU member states must achieve, *but each country can decide how to transpose these goals into national law*. Directives allow for flexibility in implementation. For example, the **General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)** started as a directive before becoming a regulation.
    
- **Regulations**: *Binding legislative acts that apply directly in all member states without the need for national implementation*. EU regulations ensure uniformity across the EU. An example is the **REACH regulation** on chemicals, which applies uniformly in all EU member states.
    
- **EU Harmonization Process**: This process ensures that national laws align with EU laws to create a cohesive legal framework across member states. Harmonization helps remove barriers to trade, enhance legal cooperation, and create a unified legal framework across different jurisdictions within the EU.
    



> [!question] When use Regulations  
> Regulations are typically used in areas where the EU needs **strong, consistent rules** to govern a specific issue, especially in matters related to the **internal market**, **competition law**, or **consumer protection**.
Example: The **General Data Protection Regulation ([[cyber law/GDPR/GDPR|GDPR]])** is an EU Regulation that applies uniformly across all member states, ensuring the same data protection standards for all citizens and businesses operating in the EU.


---

### 3. **National Sources**

- **Constitution**: The highest legal document in a state, setting out the fundamental rights of citizens and the framework for government. Constitutions supersede all other laws and are difficult to amend. For example, the **U.S. Constitution** and the **German Basic Law** set the principles for legislative, executive, and judicial functions.
    
- **Statutes (Legislation)**: Laws passed by the national legislature (parliament or congress). Statutes are below the constitution but are the primary source of law for regulating behavior in areas such as civil rights, taxation, and criminal law. For example, **civil codes** or **penal codes** fall into this category.
    
- **Administrative Regulations**: Rules issued by government agencies to implement statutes. Administrative bodies interpret and apply laws in specific contexts, such as environmental protection, labor regulations, and financial regulations. These are not passed by legislatures but derive their authority from statutory law.
    

---

### 4. **Soft Law**

- **Soft Law**: These are *non-binding guidelines*, recommendations, or standards created by international or regional organizations, or within treaties, to influence behavior without the force of law. Examples include **[[cyber law/OECD|OECD]] guidelines** on corporate governance or the **UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights**.

While soft law is not legally enforceable, it can still shape national law and international relations. It often leads to hard law, as governments may adopt soft-law principles into binding legislation over time. Soft law plays a crucial role in areas like environmental policy, corporate social responsibility, and global health.
